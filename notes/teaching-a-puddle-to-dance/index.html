Teaching a puddle to dance

October 24, 2026 · Part 2 of my SES AI internship: force fields, liquids that clump, and learning from 40,000 molecular movies

Part 2 of 3. Previously: Atom is atom, bond is bond. The big picture is in the overview.

In Part 1 the molecules were sitting still for their portraits. Now we drop them into a liquid and ask a much harder question from my slides: how do molecules move in their micro-environment?

Meet the puddle

An electrolyte is the liquid inside a battery that ions swim through. The recipe is simple: salt + solvents + additives. The behavior is not. Zoom in and it’s a crowded dance floor: lithium ions grab solvent molecules as partners, anions try to cut in, and how fast everyone moves across the floor decides how well the battery charges and discharges.

The rulebook and the movie

Two words you need for this post:

  • A force field is the rulebook for how atoms push and pull on each other.
  • Molecular dynamics (MD) is playing the movie with that rulebook: you put lots of molecules in a box, apply the rules, and watch.

If the rulebook is wrong, the movie is fiction. And the existing rulebook didn’t cover some of the new electrolyte molecules we cared about. So my first job was to extend a polarizable force field (one where the electron clouds can squish in response to their neighbors, which matters a lot around small, charged ions like lithium).

The recipe:

  1. Compute quantum (DFT) reference data on small lithium and sodium clusters. Some are binary (the ion with one partner); some are ternary (solvent and anion competing for the same lithium, like two people reaching for the last slice of pizza).
  2. Fit mainly the exp-6 term, the part of the rulebook covering short-range repulsion (“personal space!”) and dispersion (the weak, sticky attraction between everything).
  3. Test in liquid MD: density, radial distribution functions (who stands next to whom), diffusion, and conductivity.

The fit that looked perfect

Here’s the plot twist. One fit looked great on the clusters. Errors low, curves lined up, very satisfying.

Then I ran the liquid. It clumped. The ions huddled together in big clusters like penguins in a blizzard, and transport ended up far from what’s measured experimentally.

I traced it back to an ether-oxygen interaction that the fit had effectively broken. Restoring it brought the cluster fraction down from about 0.81 to 0.28, and conductivity moved closer to measurement.

The lesson, which I now believe in my bones: MD validation is part of parameter development, not a final demo. Small clusters can’t tell you everything about a liquid. You have to watch the movie.

The key part is step three. Every failure gets saved as searchable memory: the conditions, the bad parameters, the symptoms, and the checks that caught it. The next time a fit starts drifting toward “penguin huddle,” there’s a record that says we’ve seen this before, here’s what it looked like.

Honest caveat: a proper fixed-budget test of whether this memory actually makes research faster is still on my to-do list. It feels faster. Feelings aren’t data.

40,000 movies

Once you can make movies, you can make a lot of them. We had about 40,000 MD trajectories × 5,000 frames each, roughly 200 million frames. Way more than any human could watch. So I asked: can a model learn what a liquid “is” from watching?

I looked at the same liquid through three views:

  • the recipe (what went into the bottle),
  • a snapshot (one frozen frame of atoms in 3D),
  • the trajectory (how things move over time).

The snapshot encoder treats each frame as a periodic graph (atoms connected to neighbors within 6 Å), and passes messages with an equivariant network, one that tracks both plain numbers and directions, so rotating the box doesn’t confuse it. Each frame becomes 128 learned + 8 geometric numbers.

The unglamorous engineering

200 million frames is a lot of frames. Two things made it tractable:

  • Neighbor search went from O(N²) to O(N): instead of every atom asking every other atom “are you close?”, each atom only checks its own neighborhood.
  • I froze the snapshot encoder and cached every frame once. The cache is about 52 GB and cost about 395 GPU-hours, one time. After that, a training step went from 12.6 s to 0.15 s.

That’s the difference between “let’s try an idea” and “let’s try an idea next week.”

What the model learned

  • Retrieval@10 (can you find the matching liquid in the top 10?) was 84.1% with the full representation versus 82.4% from static structure alone. Surprise: a single snapshot already knows a lot!
  • Conductivity R² of 0.976, close to a strong descriptor baseline.
  • Adding trajectory features improved conductivity error in 18 of 21 held-out chemistry groups. Motion helps, especially on chemistry the model hasn’t seen.

I also played with generating trajectories (rolling the movie forward with GRU, TCN, and causal-transformer models), which was fun and is a story for another day.

An electrolyte scientist sidekick

Finally, I wrapped the tools into an “electrolyte scientist” assistant: an LLM planner with a harness and a registry of tools (reading files, checking protocols, running analysis programs, keeping evidence records). You can ask it why a simulation failed, how a formulation performed, or how to prepare a run at a new temperature. The researcher stays in the loop and reviews what it proposes to try next. Sidekick, not replacement.
