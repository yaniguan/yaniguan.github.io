Atom is atom, bond is bond

October 23, 2026 · Part 1 of my SES AI internship: teaching models to read molecules from real papers, and then to see them in 3D

Part 1 of 3. Start with the overview if you haven’t.

Here’s a scene from my slides. A cartoon scientist (me, basically) looks at a drawing of a molecule.

Me: Who are you?

Molecule: NaPF6 in FEC.

Lovely. Polite molecule. Now I show it a second drawing of the same thing, in a different style.

Molecule: Same here.

Model: Hmm…

Me: Haha, the same.

Same molecule, many outfits

People want to search chemistry by drawing: sketch a structure, find every paper that mentions it. The catch is that papers draw the same molecule in wildly different styles. Thin double bonds. Atom labels the size of a dust mite. Wedge bonds. Scans that look like they went through a fax machine twice.

A chemist looks at all of these and says the thing that became my motto for the summer: “Atom is atom, bond is bond.” The style doesn’t matter. The model, at first, disagreed.

The real bottleneck

The obvious fix is “train on more drawings.” And it’s easy to generate endless drawings with chemistry software, so you can produce a model that looks amazing. (My slides had a joke here: “99.5% accuracy (reported by me).”)

But the real bottleneck wasn’t the amount of data. It was distribution shift: the gap between tidy, computer-rendered training images and messy, real paper figures. A model that aces the rendered exam can still flunk the real world.

So I went and got real data: about 38K molecule crops from papers. Since nobody hands you ground-truth labels for those, I ran four independent readers and kept a label only when 3 of 4 agreed.

Teaching the model

Model: Teach me.

I fine-tuned vision-language models (supervised fine-tuning with LoRA, a lightweight way to adapt a big model without retraining all of it). I compared 3 base models and 6 mixtures of real and synthetic images. To keep myself honest, I split the data by molecule identity and removed 55,267 benchmark molecules (by InChIKey, a chemical fingerprint) from training, so the model couldn’t just memorize the test.

The winner: GLM trained with 70% real images, reaching 59.8% exact structure on 331 figures from ACS journals, statistically comparable to MolScribe (58.3%), a strong established reader. I wrote more about the real-vs-synthetic story in an earlier note.

The DPO detour

Then I got ambitious and tried DPO, a preference-tuning method that teaches a model to prefer good answers over bad ones. The training curves were gorgeous: loss dropped from 0.69 to 0.19, preference accuracy hit 90–97%.

Exact match on the molecules it actually generated? 0–2%.

The model had learned to win the preference game, not to read molecules. A second attempt improved validity but still landed at roughly half the accuracy of plain fine-tuning. So I kept SFT and walked away with the lesson I now repeat to anyone who’ll listen: a pretty training score can miss the scientific goal.

VERDICT: allowed to say “I don’t know”

Back to the comic:

Me: You got it, go label more real molecule depictions in papers.

Model: Yes, ma’am, even though I have high confidence, there is no ground truth. Is it reliable?

Great question, model. That’s what VERDICT answers.

Instead of trusting one model, VERDICT asks my fine-tuned VLM plus three open-source readers. It compares their answers by chemical identity (InChIKey), not raw strings, because two different strings can describe the same molecule. It keeps the disagreements and the sources, and it’s allowed to abstain.

The nice part: whether the readers agree turns out to be a great signal for whether the answer is right. Agreement separates right from wrong answers with an AUROC of about 0.916, compared with 0.547 for checking how similar a redrawn image looks to the original (which is barely better than a coin flip). Retraining on what the loop collected gave mixed gains, so I report data quality and model improvement separately rather than blending them into one happy number.

From flat drawings to 3D

Reading the drawing is just step one. The full pipeline goes: 2D image → SMILES → 3D conformer → energies and forces (with machine-learned potentials like MACE and UMA).

Why bother with 3D? A molecule has several “views.” SMILES is a line of text. A graph says who’s bonded to whom. A 3D structure says where everything actually sits in space. It’s like describing a friend by their name, their family tree, or their photo.

I tested 2D alone, 3D alone, 2D+3D, and a sneaky control: 2D plus shuffled 3D, to check whether the model was really using the geometry or just enjoying extra numbers. In the controlled test, adding real 3D cut energy error by about 18.2% and volume error by about 49.4%. Geometry matters.

Two more humbling findings:

  • Cross-view retrieval ≠ property prediction. Being good at matching a molecule’s SMILES to its 3D shape doesn’t mean you’re good at predicting what it does.
  • I trained a SMILES “student” to copy a 3D “teacher.” It matched the teacher’s vectors nicely, and gave no gain on conductivity. Copying the homework isn’t the same as understanding it.

What I took away

Real data beats more synthetic data. Agreement is a better trust signal than confidence. And 3D helps, but only if you check that it helps the thing you care about.

Molecules don’t live alone, though. Next, they jump into a liquid and start dancing: Teaching a puddle to dance.
