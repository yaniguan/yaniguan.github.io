A summer of molecules, puddles, and batteries

October 26, 2026 · Overview of a three-part series on my Applied Scientist internship at SES AI: molecules make up electrolytes, electrolytes go into batteries, and each part builds on the representations of the one before

For my internship I left my UCLA computational chemistry desk for a while and joined SES AI, a battery company, as an Applied Scientist intern, and followed one question up three scales: molecules, the electrolytes they make up, and the cells those electrolytes go into. Along the way I taught models to read molecules off real paper figures (59.8% exact on 331 journal figures), to watch about 40,000 molecular-dynamics movies of electrolytes, and to learn how a battery cell ages step by step instead of guessing one lifetime number. This post is the map; the three parts are the territory.

The one question

The official title I gave the work was “multiscale multimodal intelligence across molecules, electrolytes, and cells,” which is a mouthful, so here’s the version I’d tell you over coffee. Today’s AI models are very good with pictures and text. They can describe a cat photo and write a poem about it. But chemistry and batteries don’t really live in pictures and text. They live in 3D geometry, where atoms actually sit in space; in physical time, how those atoms jiggle, drift and react over nanoseconds to months; and in scales that connect, where a tiny change in one molecule shows up, much later, as a battery that lasts longer (or doesn’t).

So the question I kept asking all internship was whether AI can understand not just pictures and text, but 3D geometry, physical time, and how the scales connect.

Three scales, one storyline

The storyline follows the stuff itself: molecules make up electrolytes, and electrolytes go into cells. Think of a battery like a city. The molecules are the people. The electrolyte is the crowd, the liquid the ions swim through, made of salt, solvents and additives. The cell is the whole city, with its traffic jams, heat waves and aging infrastructure. You can’t understand a city from one person’s ID card, and you can’t understand a crowd without watching it move, so the work split into three parts, each building on the last.

I kept redrawing the project map all summer. The left side is molecules, from paper drawings to 3D geometry and energies. The right side is electrolytes, from quantum reference data to force fields, and from 40K MD trajectories to learned representations, both feeding an electrolyte co-scientist.

Part 1: molecules, the foundation

Part 1 starts from a plain problem: right now, mostly humans can read the molecule drawings in papers, and papers draw the same molecule in a dozen outfits, sometimes by hand. A chemist shrugs and says “atom is atom, bond is bond.” A model trained on neat, computer-rendered drawings panics.

I post-trained vision-language models to read real paper figures and found that the real bottleneck is the gap between rendered and real images. I built VERDICT, four independent readers that would rather say “I’m not sure” than make something up, and a rotation-aware recognizer that knows a rotated drawing is the same molecule but a mirrored one might not be. Then I turned recognized molecules into vectors, using 1D, 2D and 3D descriptions, five public base models and three kinds of training, and asked honestly what 3D really adds.

It all ends in a truly multimodal database, delivered through the MU product and a Chrome extension: start from a molecule picture and jump to what the paper says, its DFT properties, or its MD properties. Read it here: Atom is atom, bond is bond.

Part 2: electrolytes, molecules mixed into a liquid

Part 2 takes the molecules from Part 1 and makes them the salt, solvents and additives of an electrolyte, which looks like a boring puddle and is really a dance floor. Lithium ions grab solvent partners, anions cut in, everything shuffles.

To simulate that dance you need a rulebook, a force field, and a movie projector, molecular dynamics. I extended a polarizable force field (PFF) to 627+ molecules and 20+ anions, got humbled when fits that looked perfect on small clusters made the liquid clump (more than 50 of 80 versions failed), and distilled those failures into PFF RSI, a fitting loop that turns every saved mistake into an automatic check.

Then I taught a model to watch about 40,000 of these movies through three heads: the recipe (what’s in the bottle), a snapshot (one frozen frame of atoms) and the trajectory (how everything moves over time), all compressed into one shared 128-number space used for retrieval, alignment and property prediction.

Finally, I curated everything I’d learned (task records, simulation logs and bugs, tool definitions) to fine-tune an open-weight LLM and put it inside a harness of tools and checks: an electrolyte co-scientist that explains failed runs and prepares new ones, with a researcher reviewing what it proposes. Read it here: Teaching a puddle to dance.

Part 3: cells, electrolytes inside batteries

Part 3 puts the electrolyte formulations from Part 2 into cells and reaches the question everyone at a battery company actually cares about: if I change the recipe, say by adding an additive, what happens to the battery? Does it cycle longer, store better, charge faster?

The central idea is that nothing from Parts 1 and 2 gets thrown away. Both were mostly representation learning, so a cell can be represented by stacking the learned molecule representations and the electrolyte latent, with the cell’s design and conditions on top. Real experiments are slow and expensive, so around a 252-formulation additive experiment I built a ladder of simulations, one rung per scale: from electrons (DFT) to the liquid (MD), to bonds breaking at the electrode surface (interface reactions), to how that surface evolves over long times (kMC), to voltage, capacity and aging (P2D / SPMe), all the way up to temperature and stress across the whole cell (3D fields). Then I aligned those scales in one shared space and found that test conditions are the glue that holds them together.

The part I’m most excited about is the aging world model. Instead of a black box that predicts lifetime as one number, it learns the whole evolution process: given the cell’s design, its current state and how it’s being used, predict the next state, and roll that forward into a full aging curve. Learning the process rather than the endpoint is also where I started to see what I’d call knowledge emergence, which I’m still working to confirm properly.

It ends with a cell co-scientist that reasons over representations from all three parts, plus a decision API that hands the agent’s small, repetitive judgment calls to a typed decision model so the reasoning model can focus on reasoning. Read it here: From molecules to batteries.

The same four steps at every scale

Only when I laid the three parts side by side did I notice they follow the same four steps: represent, align, predict, and hand the result to a co-scientist. For molecules, that’s VLMs and VERDICT reading drawings plus 1D/2D/3D representations, aligned views, property prediction, and the multimodal database. For electrolytes, it’s three heads into a 128-d latent, then properties, then the electrolyte co-scientist. For cells, it’s stacked representations and multiscale alignment, then regression and classification heads plus the world model, then the cell co-scientist.

One lesson also kept showing up at every scale: a pretty number on the training objective is not the same as the scientific goal. A preference model can score beautifully and still get molecules wrong. A force field can nail tiny clusters and still ruin the liquid. An alignment can match molecules perfectly and still say almost nothing about real batteries. So most of my summer was spent building the checks: agreement between independent readers, liquid simulations inside the fitting loop, calibration against real measurements. Less glamorous than a leaderboard, but it’s what lets you trust the answer.

What this got us

I started from batteries and ended up with a method. Batteries were just where I happened to begin; the method should transfer to most systems like them, the ones built as molecules, then mixtures, then devices, where the important structure lives in drawings, geometry and simulations rather than text, and where experiments are scarce but simulation is possible. It’s still a living method, and I keep adding pieces as the field moves. Grab a snack: Part 1 starts at the foundation, with a molecule and a computer trying to figure out who it is.
