A summer of molecules, puddles, and batteries

October 26, 2026 · An overview of my Applied Scientist internship at SES AI, told across three scales

For my internship I left my UCLA computational chemistry desk for a while and joined SES AI, a battery company, as an Applied Scientist intern. If you asked me what I worked on, the honest one-line answer is: a lot of things that turned out to be one thing.

The official title I gave the work was multiscale multimodal intelligence across molecules, electrolytes, and cells. That’s a mouthful, so here’s the version I’d tell you over coffee.

The one question

Today’s AI models are very good with pictures and text. They can describe a cat photo and write a poem about it. But chemistry and batteries don’t really live in pictures and text. They live in:

  • 3D geometry: where atoms actually sit in space,
  • physical time: how those atoms jiggle, drift, and react over nanoseconds to months,
  • scales that connect: a tiny change in one molecule showing up, much later, as a battery that lasts longer (or doesn’t).

So the question I kept asking all internship was: can AI understand not just pictures and text, but 3D geometry, physical time, and how the scales connect?

Three scales, one ladder

Think of a battery like a city. The molecules are the people. The electrolyte is the crowd: the liquid the ions swim through, made of salt, solvents, and additives. The cell is the whole city, with its traffic jams, heat waves, and aging infrastructure.

You can’t understand a city from one person’s ID card, and you can’t understand a crowd without watching it move. So the work naturally split into three parts, each climbing one rung of the ladder.

Part 1: molecules (or, “atom is atom, bond is bond”)

People want to search for molecules by drawing them. Papers, unfortunately, draw the same molecule in a dozen outfits: thin double bonds, tiny atom labels, wedges, blurry scans. A human chemist shrugs and says “atom is atom, bond is bond.” A model trained on neat, computer-rendered drawings panics.

I fine-tuned vision-language models to read real paper figures, found that the real bottleneck is the gap between rendered training images and real figures, took a doomed detour through preference optimization, and ended up building a system that would rather say “I’m not sure” than make something up. Then I followed the molecules from 2D drawings all the way into 3D.

👉 Atom is atom, bond is bond

Part 2: electrolytes (or, teaching a puddle to dance)

An electrolyte looks like a boring puddle. Zoom in and it’s a dance floor: lithium ions grabbing solvent partners, anions cutting in, everything shuffling. To simulate that dance you need a rulebook (a force field) and a movie projector (molecular dynamics).

I extended a polarizable force field to new electrolyte molecules, got humbled when a fit that looked perfect on small clusters made the liquid clump, and built a loop that remembers its own failures. Then I taught a model to watch ~40,000 of these movies and learn what a liquid “looks like” from its recipe, a single snapshot, and its motion over time.

👉 Teaching a puddle to dance

Part 3: cells (or, from molecules to batteries)

The question everyone in a battery company actually cares about: if I change the recipe (say, add an additive), what happens to the battery? Does it cycle longer, store better, charge faster?

Real experiments are slow and expensive, so I built a ladder of simulations, from quantum chemistry up to whole-cell heat maps, tried to align all those scales into one shared space, and trained a “world model” that predicts how a cell ages, step by step. The most useful outcomes were honest ones: what connects, what doesn’t, and where the model is too confident.

👉 From molecules to batteries

The thread that ties it together

If there’s one lesson that kept showing up at every scale, it’s this: a pretty number on the training objective is not the same as the scientific goal. A preference model can score beautifully and still get molecules wrong. A force field can nail tiny clusters and still ruin the liquid. An alignment can match molecules perfectly and still say almost nothing about real batteries.

So most of my summer was spent building the checks: agreement between independent readers, liquid simulations as part of the fitting loop, calibration against real measurements. Less glamorous than a leaderboard, but it’s what lets you trust the answer.
