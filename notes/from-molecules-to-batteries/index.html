From molecules to batteries

October 25, 2026 · Part 3 of my SES AI internship: a simulation ladder, multiscale alignment, and a world model for battery aging

Part 3 of 3. Previously: Atom is atom, bond is bond and Teaching a puddle to dance. The big picture is in the overview.

We’ve read molecules off paper and watched them dance in a liquid. Now for the question that actually pays the bills at a battery company.

Δrecipe → Δperformance

If I change the recipe, say by adding a pinch of some additive, what changes in the battery? Does it survive more charge cycles? Store better on a shelf? Charge faster? Handle high currents?

It’s a bit like baking. Swap one ingredient and the cake might rise higher, or collapse, or taste fine today and go stale tomorrow. The annoying part is that each real “bake” for a battery is slow and expensive, so real experiments are limited. Compute, on the other hand, is available. The plan: use simulations to fill in the gaps, while real measurements stay the reference. Simulations get to advise; they don’t get to overrule.

A ladder of simulations, in about a month

No single simulation covers everything from electrons to a whole cell heating up, so I built a high-throughput ladder in about a month. Each rung zooms out:

  • DFT: local chemistry and electron transfer. Who gives electrons to whom.
  • MD: the liquid, using the force fields from Part 2.
  • Interface reactions: what happens where the liquid meets the electrode, the battery’s busiest border crossing.
  • kMC (kinetic Monte Carlo): states, events, and rates, like a board game where each move happens with a certain probability per unit time.
  • P2D/SPMe and 3D thermal fields: the whole cell, including temperature and stress.

Can we line up all the scales?

With data from every rung, the natural next idea was to align them, CLIP-style. CLIP is the trick that taught models to put a photo and its caption close together in one shared space. Here, the “photo” and “caption” are a molecule, its liquid, its interface, and its cell. I used a multi-positive InfoNCE loss, which is a fancy way of saying “pull together everything that belongs to the same experiment, push apart everything that doesn’t.”

Two findings, one encouraging and one sobering:

  1. Conditions are essential. Temperature, test protocol, and the like are the glue between scales. When I removed them, the MD-to-electrode link score collapsed from 0.327 to 0.066. Without knowing how a battery was tested, the scales stop recognizing each other.
  2. Molecules ↛ battery just via alignment. Matching a molecule’s identity across views could be strong while the links to real experiments stayed weak. Knowing exactly who someone is doesn’t tell you how they’ll do at their new job.

A world model for aging

Batteries are a story told over time: they age, cycle by cycle. So instead of predicting the ending directly, I trained a world model: learn from aging simulations to predict the next state, then roll it out, feeding each prediction back in as the next input. Like predicting tomorrow’s weather, then using that forecast to predict the day after.

That’s a harsh test, because small errors snowball. So I checked four things: next-step accuracy, long-run drift, whether it gets the direction of change right, and calibration to real experiments.

  • I compared 41 architectures. The learned world model beat a direct MLP baseline on simulated sequences.
  • That does not by itself prove transfer to real cells. Simulated sequences are the practice field, not the match.
  • Calibration to experiments supported 24 targets.
  • The uncertainty was overconfident: nominal 90% intervals actually covered about 68–78%. When the model said “I’m 90% sure,” it should have said “eh, maybe 70%.” Good to know, and now it’s written down.

The VC mystery

My favorite moment came from one additive, VC. Its trends differed between two different cell designs: same ingredient, different story depending on the cell. The physics model reproduced some of the direction changes, but not the full size of the effect.

That sounds like a failure, but it’s actually the most useful kind of result. “The model gets the direction but not the magnitude” points at a specific missing mechanism, and that became the next mechanism test. A good model doesn’t just answer questions; it tells you which experiment to run next.

A cell co-scientist

As with the electrolytes, I wrapped the pieces into a co-scientist: an LLM that pulls in multi-scale evidence and calls tools, with chemistry-aware tokenization so chemical names get read as chemistry, not random letter soup. The interface shows every tool call and record ID, and it keeps measured, computed, and predicted values clearly separated. If you’ve read this far, you know why that last part matters to me.

Wrapping up the summer

Across molecules, electrolytes, and cells, the same pattern kept repeating: the exciting number is rarely the whole truth, and the honest checks are where the real progress hides. Agreement over confidence. Liquid tests over cluster fits. Calibration over leaderboards.
